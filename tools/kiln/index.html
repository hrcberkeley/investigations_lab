How it works

What this does: finds satellite locations that look similar to a site you pick, using precomputed Clay foundation-model embeddings over Sentinel-2 imagery.

Cost model: each parquet file is split into row groups of roughly 126 MB compressed. The tool fetches only the row groups whose bounding box overlaps your drawn area — nothing is downloaded until you confirm the cost estimate shown in the Load panel.

Coverage: the dataset is populated incrementally. The Americas and Pacific are well-covered; other regions may show "no data available" as more tiles are added. A missing tile is normal — not a tool error.

Similarity scores are cosine similarity in embedding space. A score of 1.0 means visually identical; lower scores mean less similar. Chips below the threshold slider are hidden from the map but remain in memory.

All computation runs in your browser. The only outbound traffic is HTTP range requests to public cloud storage — no data leaves your machine.

Kiln
Satellite embedding similarity & change search, powered by Clay AI

What is this tool?

Kiln is a browser-based geospatial intelligence tool that lets you search for satellite locations that look like a place you choose — or detect where the Earth's surface has changed between two dates. Everything runs in your browser; the only network traffic is range requests to a public dataset hosted on Source Cooperative.

It was built for investigative journalism, environmental monitoring, and remote-sensing research — tasks where you need to find analogous facilities, map a land-cover transition, or scan a region for unusual change without writing any code.

What are Clay embeddings?

Clay is an open-source Earth-observation foundation model trained by LGND AI on hundreds of millions of satellite tiles. Clay v1.5, the version used here, is a 632-million-parameter Vision Transformer that ingests Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery together with location and timestamp metadata and outputs a 256-dimensional embedding — a compact numeric fingerprint that mathematically encodes what a place looks like at a given moment.

The embeddings capture high-level land surface characteristics — vegetation density, urban form, water bodies, bare earth, agricultural patterns — without any manual labeling. LGND AI applied Clay v1.5 across the full global Sentinel-2 archive and published the result as an open dataset organized on the MajorTOM 1280-meter grid, with coverage of two snapshots: June 2024 and June 2025. A third snapshot was added in June 2026.

Because similar-looking places produce similar embeddings, you can find global matches to any reference location by comparing embedding vectors — no training, no labels, no GPU required.

What can you do with it?

Similarity search: draw an area, load its embeddings, click a chip on the map as your reference site. Kiln instantly ranks every loaded chip by how similar it is to your reference. Use this to find industrial facilities that match a known type, identify terrain analogues, or locate patterns of interest across a region.

Change detection: load the same area for two different time periods, switch to Change detection mode, and compute how much each cell shifted between the two snapshots. Cells are colored by magnitude of change in embedding space — a reliable proxy for land-cover transitions, construction, deforestation, or flooding.

Change-analog search: after a change run, click any changed cell and search for everywhere that underwent the same kind of transition — useful for mapping a process (e.g. vegetation → bare earth) across a wide region.

Concept probe: teach the tool a concept by example. Switch to Concept mode, paint a handful of chips that are the thing you're after (e.g. a tailings pond) and a handful that are not, then train. Kiln fits a small linear classifier (logistic regression) on the embeddings in your browser in milliseconds and scores every loaded chip by how well it matches the concept you defined — turning "similar to this one place" into "matches this idea". Tick Show only held-out regions to train on one region and evaluate on another for an honest test.

How to interpret results

Scores are cosine similarity between two 256-dimensional unit vectors. A score of 1.0 means the two chips are pointing in exactly the same direction in embedding space — essentially identical land cover. Use the table below as a rough guide:

Score rangeWhat it means
0.92 – 1.00Very high similarity — near-identical land surface character. Strong match.
0.80 – 0.91High similarity — same broad land-cover class with noticeable variation.
0.65 – 0.79Moderate — related but distinct character. Worth reviewing.
< 0.65Likely dissimilar — treat as a weak or coincidental match.

Treat scores as relative rankings within a result set, not absolute thresholds. The default slider filters out chips below 50 % — adjust it to widen or narrow the result set. For change detection, scores represent cosine distance (how much the embedding shifted), so higher means more change.
